L8:The dynamic process by which bone marrow-derived monocytes differentiate into macrophages while migrating across the vascular endothelium is inherently complex during bacterial neuroinvasion33. This transition is accompanied by marked morphological and functional changes, including increased cell size, development of cytoplasmic projections, upregulation of macrophage markers such as F4/80, and acquisition of enhanced phagocytic capacity. In this context, the gating criteria of CD36+ subpopulations were applied consistently throughout this study: circulating CD36+ monocytes were defined as CD11b+CD64+CD36+ cells within the PBMC compartment, whereas CD36+ macrophages in brain tissue were defined as CD45+CD11b+F4/80+CD36+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 2c). In order to gain insight into the characteristics of CD36+ subpopulations, CD36+ and CD36− cells were isolated from peripheral blood. In comparison to CD36− cells, the CD36+ subpopulations exhibited a markedly elevated level of granularity (SSChigh; Supplementary Fig. 2c), indicative of an increased number of proteins or organelles within their intracellular compartment and the presence of protrusions on their surfaces. The sorted monocytes were cultured in vitro overnight and then labeled with BODIPY FL, a lipophilic probe for polar and neutral lipids. The results showed that the CD36+ subpopulations exhibited a significantly greater accumulation of lipid droplets than the CD36− cells (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Furthermore, Oil Red O staining was conducted to corroborate this finding (Supplementary Fig. 2d). To confirm the lipid uptake ability of the CD36+ monocytes, we employed the use of fluorescently labeled 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate (Dil)-conjugated oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL), and the uptake of Dil-conjugated ox-LDL particles by CD36+ and CD36− monocytes was quantified. Our findings revealed that CD36+ cells internalized more than twice the amount of ox-LDL compared to CD36− cells (Supplementary Fig. 2e).
L12:To ascertain whether the rise in CD36+ macrophages facilitated the transfer of L. monocytogenes into the brain in vivo, we performed adoptive macrophage transfer experiments via tail vein injection based on an irradiated mouse model (Fig. 2e). Bone marrow irradiation-based immunosuppression resulted in mice being more susceptible to bacterial infection, which necessitated a reduction of the infectious dose to 1 × 106 CFU of L. monocytogenes. Under these conditions, intravenously transferred, bacteria-loaded CD36+ macrophages were found to localize predominantly to cerebral blood vessels (Supplementary Fig. 3j). Moreover, mice receiving CD36+ macrophages exhibited shortened survival, greater body weight loss, and significantly worsened clinical scores compared with control mice (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 3k, l). Furthermore, we showed that the brain bacterial loads in mice receiving CD36+ macrophages were significantly higher than those in mice receiving CD36− macrophages (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 3m). Interestingly, no significant differences were observed in bacterial loads among the lung, spleen, and liver (Supplementary Fig. 3n). Additionally, we examined the levels of pro-inflammatory factors in the CSF and brain homogenate. The results showed a notable elevation in interleukin 6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) levels (Fig. 2h, i), indicating a more severe encephalitis phenotype in the CD36+ macrophage transfer group. Importantly, the brains of mice transferred with CD36+ macrophages exhibited no discernible Evans blue extravasation, suggesting that these macrophages facilitated the transfer of L. monocytogenes into the brain without disrupting the BBB (Supplementary Fig. 3o, p). The results of the pathological histology analysis confirmed the existence of more severe inflammation and neuronal vacuolation in the cerebral cortices of the mice transferred with CD36+ macrophages than in the control group (Supplementary Fig. 3o, q). Furthermore, when mice were infected with a relatively low dose (1 × 105 CFU/mouse) of L. monocytogenes, the difference in bacterial load in brain tissues between the CD36+ macrophage transferred mice and the control group was also observed at day 2 post infection (Supplementary Fig. 3r–t). The adoptive transfer experiments were also performed on a mouse model without irradiation. We found that recipients that received CD36+ macrophages displayed significantly reduced survival and markedly higher bacterial burdens in the brain compared with mice receiving CD36- macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 3u–w). These findings were consistent with the data obtained using the irradiation-based model. Notably, mice treated with CD36 blocking antibodies exhibited significantly improved survival and markedly reduced bacterial burdens in the brain compared with control-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 3u–w).
