L1:Macrophage-derived amphiregulin induces myofibroblast transition in adipogenic lineage precursors near  Staphylococcus aureus  abscess in bone marrow
L8:The formation of  Staphylococcus aureus  ( S. aureus ) abscesses is a well-established determinant of persistent skeletal infections, yet the mechanisms underlying bacterial persistence remain elusive. Here, we demonstrate that bone marrow adiponectin-positive ( Adipoq + ) precursors are mobilized to surround  S. aureus  abscesses and undergo myofibroblast differentiation. This phenotypic transition induces vascular constriction, thereby impairing local perfusion and impeding effective bacterial clearance. Mechanistically, macrophage-derived amphiregulin (AREG) activates EGFR signaling on  Adipoq +  cells, triggering the mTOR/YAP pathway to drive their myofibroblast transition. Importantly, genetic ablation of  Adipoq +  cells, cell-specific deletion of the AREG/EGFR axis, or pharmacological inhibition of EGFR/mTOR signaling effectively alleviates fibrosis, restores vascular perfusion and antibiotic delivery, and promotes bacterial eradication from abscesses. Our findings implicate a macrophage- Adipoq +  cell regulatory axis that sustains  S. aureus  persistence in osteomyelitis and identify therapeutic targeting of this axis as a strategy to enhance antibiotic efficacy against  S. aureus  skeletal infections.
L10:Infection; Bacterial infection; Osteoimmunology; Bone marrow; Monocytes and macrophages
L13:The persistence of  S. aureus  in skeletal infections is driven by several factors, such as biofilms associated with implants, abscess formation, intracellular infections, and invasion into the osteocyte lacuno-canalicular network 12 . Recent clinical data highlight that local abscess formation significantly escalates the risk of treatment failure in  S. aureus  bone infections 13 . These deep-tissue  S. aureus  abscess communities, within bone marrow, not only inhibit the bactericidal activity of nearby neutrophils and macrophages 14 , 15 , but also facilitate spread and creation of new infection sites, becoming a sustaining source of chronic bone infection 16 , 17 . Although the cellular composition of  S. aureus  abscesses within the bone marrow is well documented 1 , 12 , 18 , 19 , the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying antibiotic failure in these  S. aureus  abscess communities remain poorly understood.
L14:Here, we report that adiponectin-positive ( Adipoq + ) cells, a type of marrow adipogenic lineage precursors (MALPs) in bone marrow, which predominantly regulate bone marrow homeostasis and support macrophage development, osteoclastogenesis, and hematopoietic stem cell regeneration 20 – 22 , are important for myofibroblast formation adjacent to  S. aureus  abscesses. This, in turn, significantly restricts blood perfusion to the infected areas. Moreover, we have identified that amphiregulin (AREG), secreted by bone marrow macrophages, is pivotal in initiating the myofibroblast transition in  Adipoq +  cells by stimulating the EGFR/mTOR/YAP signaling pathway. Our investigations further show that targeted deletion of AREG in bone marrow macrophages, ablation of EGFR in  Adipoq +  cells, or pharmacological inhibition of EGFR/mTOR signaling effectively decreases myofibroblast development, enhances vascular perfusion and antibiotics delivery, and subsequently diminishes bacterial burden and mitigates bone destruction in  S. aureus -induced osteomyelitis. These findings elucidate critical mechanisms promoting  staphylococcal  abscess pathogenesis during osteomyelitis progression, providing a foundation for developing targeted antifibrotic therapies that may potentiate antibiotic efficacy against  S. aureus  infections in the skeleton.

L21:A hallmark of abscesses is the formation of a fibrous capsule at the periphery 24 , which can impede tissue perfusion by compressing blood vessels 25 . Masson’s trichrome staining highlighted a notable increase in collagen deposition around the abscess in femoral bone marrow affected by  S. aureus -induced osteomyelitis by days 7 and 14 post-infection (Fig.  S1B ,  C ). Immunostaining further confirmed progressively elevated protein levels of the actin filament marker α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and the extracellular matrix component marker type I collagen (Col-I) around the abscess from day 3–14 post-infection. Notably,  Cdh5 +  blood vessels surrounding the abscess showed extensive encapsulation by collagen fibers and complete luminal occlusion (Fig.  1D–F ). Importantly, at 7 and 14 days post-infection, FITC-conjugated gentamicin (FITC-gentamicin) distribution in infected femurs was primarily observed in extra-abscess regions, with progressively diminished penetration into abscesses (Fig.  S2A ,  B ), indicating that the compression of vessels can hinder antibiotics penetration and effectiveness, potentially exacerbating the infection. Thus, despite treatment with  S. aureus -sensitive antibiotics in the mouse models, substantial bacterial accumulation occurred around the implant site and small abscesses emerged in the femurs by day 7 (Fig.  S2C ,  D ). Infections persisted, and by day 14, multiple abscesses formed and some fused into a large abscess that dominated the bone marrow cavity (Figs.  S1B ,  C  and   S2C ,  D ). These findings suggest that fibrosis formation around the abscess might be a key factor for the progression of  S. aureus  osteomyelitis.

L25:By days 7 and 14 post-infection,  Adipoq +  cells exhibited significantly increased α-SMA and Col-I expression, suggesting a myofibroblast transition (Fig.  2A–C , Fig.  S4 ). To further investigate the role of  Adipoq +  cells during  S. aureus  osteomyelitis, we generated an osteomyelitis model on  AdipoqCre;Rosa26-iDTR  ( AdipoqCre-iDTR ) mice. Immunofluorescence staining revealed a striking reduction in Adiponectin + α-SMA +  and Adiponectin + Col-I +  double-positive cells surrounding abscess in the infected femurs of diphtheria toxin (DT)-treated  AdipoqCre-iDTR  mice, compared with either DT-treated  iDTR  mice or vehicle-treated  AdipoqCre-iDTR  mice (Fig.  2D–F ). This observation corresponded with improvements in blood vessel lumen size and Endomucin (Emcn) + Lectin +  area, evidenced by fluorescein-conjugated lectin injection and Emcn immunofluorescence (Fig.  2G–I ), suggesting enhanced blood flow near infection sites in DT-treated  AdipoqCre-iDTR  mice. Excitingly, DT-treated  AdipoqCre-iDTR  mice showed a significant reduction in bacterial load in both infected femurs and implants by day 14 (Fig.  2J–L  and  S5A ), along with a substantial decrease in  S. aureus  colonization areas compared with both control groups (Figs.  S5B , C). Notably, at 28 days post infection when osteomyelitis typically progresses to chronic stage 27 , 28 , bacterial loads in DT-treated  AdipoqCre-iDTR  mice exhibited further reduction in both infected femurs and implants compared with day 14 post-infection (Figs.  2J–L  and  S5A ). In contrast, both control groups (DT-treated  iDTR  mice and vehicle-treated  AdipoqCre-iDTR  mice) harbored progressively increasing bacterial burdens from day 14 to day 28 post-infection (Figs.  2J–L  and  S5A ). These findings demonstrate that ablation of myofibroblastic  Adipoq +  cells enhances bacterial clearance in mouse osteomyelitis. L26:Histopathology supported the beneficial effects of removing  Adipoq +  cells on femoral bone integrity in mice with  S. aureus  osteomyelitis. At day 14 post-infection, DT-treated  AdipoqCre-iDTR  mice displayed markedly reduced histopathological scores with preserved femoral architecture and limited abscesses formation, compared with both DT-treated  iDTR  mice and vehicle-treated  AdipoqCre-iDTR  mice (Fig.  S5D ,  E ). Micro-CT analysis confirmed preserved microstructure in infected femurs of DT-treated  AdipoqCre-iDTR  mice at both days 14 and 28 post-infection (Fig.  S6A – H ). Quantitative analysis revealed progressive cortical bone loss coupled with increasing reactive bone formation in both controls (DT-treated  iDTR  mice and vehicle-treated  AdipoqCre-iDTR  mice) from day 14 to day 28 post-infection. In contrast, DT-treated  AdipoqCre-iDTR  mice exhibited significantly attenuated cortical bone destruction during the same period (Fig.  S6B ,  C ). These DT-treated  AdipoqCre-iDTR  mice also showed improvements in both bone mineral density (BMD) and bone volume fraction (BV/TV) from day 14 to day 28 post-infection, primarily attributed to enhanced trabecular number (Tb. N) and improved trabecular bone pattern factor (Tb. Pf), rather than trabecular thickness (Tb. Th) (Fig.  S6A  and Fig.  S6D – H ). These results suggest that  Adipoq +  cells might contribute to myofibroblasts formation around  S. aureus  abscess, consequently exacerbating osteomyelitis progression.
L27:Given that  Adipoq +  cells are an important niche component for bone marrow hematopoiesis 29 , 30 , genetic ablation of them or their phenotype alterations under  S. aureus  infection could potentially alter the local effector immune response. Consequently, we performed flow cytometry analyses to quantify the immune cell populations and ROS levels in neutrophils and macrophages from both DT-treated  AdipoqCre-iDTR  mice and DT-treated  iDTR  control mice, with or without  S. aureus  osteomyelitis in the femurs. Whether in non-infected or infected states,  Adipoq +  cell depletion did not alter the proportion of neutrophils, macrophages, and dendritic cells, but it induced a moderate reduction in monocytes under  S. aureus -infected conditions (Fig.  S7A –F ). Notably, in the  S. aureus  osteomyelitis model, ablation of  Adipoq +  cells reduced both T and B cell populations (Fig.  S8A – D ), while neutrophil and macrophage ROS production remained unaffected (Fig.  S9A – D ). These findings imply that mechanisms other than localized immune responses likely contribute to the enhanced bacterial clearance observed after  Adipoq +  cell ablation.
L28:Next, we tested whether  S. aureus  infection directly triggers myofibroblastic transition in preadipocytes. Unexpectedly,  S. aureus  infection had no effect on the mRNA levels of myofibroblast marker genes ( Acta2 ,  Tagln ,  Col-I ,  Lox  and  Loxl2 ) in 3T3-L1 adipocyte precursor cell line (Fig.  S10A ). These results were validated in primary mouse bone marrow preadipocytes (BMpreA), with over 90% of differentiated cells expressing characteristic preadipocyte markers adiponectin and stem cell factor (SCF) (Fig.  S10B – D ). Furthermore, we didn’t observe the intracellular colocalization of  S. aureus  in  Adipoq +  cells in vivo (Fig.  S10E ). Collectively, these findings indicate that the myofibroblast transition of  Adipoq +  cells in  S. aureus -induced osteomyelitis occurs indirectly, likely mediated by abscess microenvironment factors rather than a direct influence of bacterial infection.
L30:It is well established that neutrophils primarily aggregate in the center, while macrophages are localized at the periphery of  S. aureus  abscess 18 . In  S. aureus  osteomyelitis model generated using  Lyz2Cre-tdTomato  mice, we observed substantial peripheral accumulations of  Lyz2 + F4/80 +  macrophages by day 3 post-infection, with a further significant increase by days 7 and 14 (Fig.  S11A, B ), mirroring  Adipoq +  cells in both temporal and spatial distribution. We next investigated the possibility that macrophages initiate the myofibroblast transition of  Adipoq +  cells following  S. aureus  infection. We found that the conditional medium of primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) exposed to  S. aureus  ( S. aureus -MФ CM) substantially up-regulated the mRNA levels of myofibroblast marker genes ( Acta2 ,  Tagln ,  Col1a1 ,  Lox  and  Loxl2 ) in both 3T3-L1 preadipocytes and primary mouse BMpreA compared with control treatments (Fig.  S11C, D ).

L32:To identify the pivotal factors involved in myofibroblast transition of  Adipoq +  in bone during  S. aureus  osteomyelitis, we revisited the published transcriptome data of  S. aureus-  and mock-infected mouse femurs 27 . Gene Ontology (GO) analysis identified several enriched biological processes among the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including extracellular matrix organization, cytokine activity, growth factor activity, and myofibroblast transition-associated signaling (Fig.  S11E ). Of these, some genes such as  Areg ,  Tgfb2 ,  Tgfb3  and  Bmp5  were the common genes at the intersection of cytokine, growth factor, and myofibroblast transition pathways (Fig.  3A ). Next, we examine whether  S. aureus  challenge may evoke the mRNA levels of the above genes in BMDMs as well as other genes that had been demonstrated to be involved in regulating myofibroblast transition, such as  Ctgf ,  Pdgfa ,  Tgfb1 ,  Tgfa ,  Hbegf  and  Egf . To our surprise, only  Areg  and  Hbegf  genes were significantly activated upon  S. aureus  challenge (Fig.  S11F ). ELISA data confirmed a parallel increase in AREG and HBEGF protein levels post-infection in BMDMs (Fig.  S11G ). Despite this, only AREG was effective in up-regulating myofibroblast markers in both 3T3-L1 preadipocytes and primary mouse BMpreA after 48 hours (Fig.  3B, C ), whereas HBEGF had no such effect (Fig.  S11H, I ). L33:Neutrophils, monocytes and macrophages are among the frontline responders in  S. aureus  infection and abscess formation 12 , 31 . In the mouse primary BMDMs, the mRNA level of  Areg  was strikingly upregulated in a time-dependent manner after  S. aureus  exposure. However, there were much lower or non-existent increases in monocytes and neutrophils isolated from mouse bone marrow, respectively (Fig.  S11J ). To confirm the cellular source of AREG in vivo, we performed flow cytometry to quantify AREG levels in innate immune cell populations, including CD11b + Ly6G +  neutrophils, CD11b + F4/80 +  macrophages, CD11b + Ly6C +  monocytes, and CD11c + MHCII +  dendritic cells. Results revealed a significant upregulation of AREG specifically in CD11b + F4/80 +  macrophages from the bone marrow of  S. aureus -infected femurs (Fig.  S12A – D ). In contrast, AREG levels remained unchanged in neutrophils and dendritic cells, while showing moderate downregulation in monocytes (Fig.  S12A – H ). These findings demonstrate that macrophages serve as the primary cellular source of AREG in  S. aureus- infected bone marrow.
L34:Further analysis revealed that cytochalasin D, a phagocytosis inhibitor, significantly suppressed  S. aureus -induced AREG mRNA levels in BMDMs (Fig.  S13A ), indicating that AREG upregulation in macrophages depends primarily on internalized  S. aureus . Considering the role of AREG as a ligand for the epithelial growth factor receptor (EGFR) 32 , we examined the effect of gefitinib, an EGFR inhibitor, on 3T3-L1 preadipocytes. We found that gefitinib completely blocked the induction of mRNA levels of myofibroblast marker genes by  S. aureus -MФ CM (Fig.  S13B ). These findings implicate the critical role of macrophage-derived AREG in myofibroblast transition of preadipocytes.
L35:In the  S. aureus  osteomyelitis model generated using  Lyz2Cre-tdTomato  mice, the number of  Lyz2 + AREG +  cells surrounding the abscess increased significantly by days 7 and 14 post-infection (Fig.  3D, E ). We then asked whether knock-out of  Areg  in  Lyz2 +  macrophages could rescue the myofibroblast transition around  S. aureus  abscess. In the  S. aureus  osteomyelitis model generated using  Lyz2Cre-Areg f/f  mice, immunofluorescence staining showed that knocking out  Areg  in  Lyz2 +  cells markedly reduced the proportion of Adiponectin + α-SMA +  and Adiponectin + Col-I +  cells around  S. aureus  abscess (Fig.  3F–H , Fig.  S14A ). Consistent with the rescued myofibroblast transition surrounding  S. aureus  abscess, vascular analyses revealed a substantial increase in the luminal area of the Emcn-stained blood vessels and the Emcn + Lectin +  area surrounding  S. aureus  abscess of  Lyz2Cre-Areg f/f  mice compared with that of  Areg f/f  mice (Fig.  3I–K , Fig.  S14B ). The recovery of blood perfusion will benefit sustained delivery of antibiotics to the infection site, thereby alleviating bacterial burden. Indeed, we observed markedly higher levels of FITC-gentamicin within the abscess of infected femurs in  Lyz2Cre-Areg f/f  mice compared with  Areg f/f  mice (Fig.  3L, M ). Moreover,  Lyz2Cre-Areg f/f  mice exhibited significantly lower bacterial loads in both infected femurs and implants (Fig.  3N, O , Fig.  S14C ), and markedly reduced  S. aureus -positive areas in infected femurs compared with  Areg f/f  mice (Fig.  S14D , E). Likewise, scanning electron micrographs demonstrated significantly fewer scattered adherent  S. aureus  on the surface of implants from  Lyz2Cre-Areg f/f  mice compared with those from  Areg f/f  mice (Fig.  S14F ,  G ).

L64:To determine whether mTOR inhibition could attenuate myofibroblast transition and disease progression in  S. aureus  osteomyelitis, we treated infected  AdipoqCre-tdTomato  mice with rapamycin. Immunofluorescence staining showed a markedly reduced  Adipoq + YAP +  double-positive cells surrounding the  S. aureus  abscess following rapamycin treatment (Fig.  7G–I ). Importantly, despite the presence of  Adipoq +  cells recruited around the  S. aureus  abscess in rapamycin-treated mice, there was a considerable reduction in the number of  Adipoq + α-SMA +  and  Adipoq + Col-I +  cells compared with vehicle-treated mice (Fig.  7J–L ). Due to the reduced formation of myofibroblasts around abscess, both the  Cdh5 + -labeled vascular lumen area and  Cdh5 + Lectin +  area were substantially improved in rapamycin-treated mice (Fig.  7M–O ). The improved blood perfusion around abscess may potentially enhance antibiotic delivery and bacterial clearance, evidenced by the improved distribution of FITC-gentamicin in abscesses (Fig.   S26A, B ), a significant reduction in bacterial load in infected femurs and implants (Fig.  7P–R ), and a notably decreased  S. aureus +  area in the infected femurs of rapamycin-treated mice (Fig.  S26C, D ). These findings correlated with mitigated pathological changes, including reduced abscess formation, less reactive new bone formation around cortical bone, and limited trabecular bone loss in the metaphyseal region in  S. aureus -infected femurs of rapamycin-treated mice (Fig.  S27A, B ). Micro-CT data confirmed that rapamycin-treated mice had considerably improved bone structure, characterized by notably improved microarchitecture of both cortical bone and trabecular bone (Fig.  S27C – J ). Together, our findings demonstrate that the mTOR-YAP axis acts as a downstream effector of AREG-EGFR signaling, driving myofibroblast transition of  Adipoq +  cells around abscess, playing a pivotal role in the progression of  S. aureus  osteomyelitis in mice.
L66:The formation of abscesses is one of the key mechanisms for  S. aureus  persistence in skeletal infections 12 . Due to compromised blood flow in these regions, both systemic and localized antibiotic therapies exhibited limited efficacy 23 . Although aggressive surgical debridement is effective, it often leads to considerable bone defects. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying restricted blood flow near abscess is fundamentally important for developing innovative therapeutic approaches. Here, we have identified a macrophage-to-MALPs signaling pathway that orchestrates the formation of  S. aureus  abscess, thereby promotes progress of osteomyelitis. Through rigorous in vivo genetic studies, we demonstrate that MALPs, bone marrow adipocyte precursors, are recruited to the  S. aureus  abscess and converted into myofibroblasts, constituting a major component restricting blood perfusion. Molecularly, AREG-EGFR signaling mediates the crosstalk between bone marrow macrophages and MALPs, activating myofibroblast transition of  Adipoq +  cells through the mTOR/YAP pathway. Importantly, genetic ablation of  Adipoq +  cells, knockdown of AREG-EGFR axis, or pharmacological inhibition of the EGFR-mTOR pathway effectively suppresses myofibroblast genesis, enhances blood perfusion, and consequently promotes bacterial clearance within abscess. This study addresses a major knowledge gap in understanding the fibrotic process surrounding abscess and its role in  S. aureus- induced osteomyelitis progression. We propose that targeting macrophage-MALP crosstalk through AREG-EGFR-mTOR inhibition represents a promising therapeutic strategy to potentiate antibiotic efficacy against  S. aureus  infection in bone.
L67:A major finding in this study is the predominant role of  Adipoq +  cells play, especially their expansion and myofibroblast transition surrounding  S. aureus  abscess within bone marrow. In models of  S. aureus -infected skin or excisional wounds, these cells favor adipogenesis with increased lipid production and elevated cathelicidin-related antimicrobial peptide levels 41 , 42 . A recent study in radiation-injured bone marrow models shows that these cells expand around the injury site, subsequently are converted into myofibroblasts or differentiated into adipocytes with lipid accumulation 26 . Contrarily, our work has shown that in the bone marrow microenvironment, the expanded  Adipoq +  cells around  S. aureus  abscess preferentially transdifferentiate into myofibroblasts rather than maturing into adipocytes. Thus, it is possible that the fate of  Adipoq +  cells under pathological conditions is both tissue-specific and stimulus-dependent. Of particular significance, we demonstrate that these cells predominantly adopt a myofibroblast identity in osteomyelitis model. The pathological formation of myofibroblasts, coupled with excess extracellular matrix deposition, notably hampers the functionality of multiple organs, including the heart, lung, liver, kidney, skin, and bone marrow 43 . We extended the above concept that the myofibroblast transition of  Adipoq +  cells surrounding  S. aureus  abscess contributes substantially to decreased blood perfusion and compromised antibiotics delivery, which in turn leads to sustained infection within the bone.
L68:Another intriguing discovery from this study is the identification of bone marrow macrophages as a pivotal mediator in myofibroblast transition of  Adipoq +  cells surrounding  S. aureus  abscess. As a frontline of host defense system, macrophages have potent effector functions of phagocytosis and bacterial killing 44 , 45 . Our recent study has shown that during persistent  S. aureus  infections, bone marrow macrophages have a diminished bactericidal capacity, which correlates with a colocalization of  S. aureus  in abscess 46 . The present study further uncovers that intracellular  S. aureus  triggers a macrophage-induced profibrotic response that activates the conversion of  Adipoq +  cells into myofibroblasts. While the pivotal role of tissue-resident macrophages in initiating and perpetuating myofibroblasts is well documented, particularly in organs such as skin, lung, and liver 47 – 49 , the specific mechanisms underlying localized bone marrow fibrosis during  S. aureus  osteomyelitis remain poorly understood. Our study addresses this knowledge gap by identifying macrophages-derived AREG as an essential element for promoting myofibroblast transition of  Adipoq +  cells in the context of  S. aureus  abscess formation within bone marrow.
L69:The study has revealed an important role of AREG-EGFR signaling in mediating the myofibroblast transition of  Adipoq +  cells during  S. aureus  osteomyelitis in mice. While AREG can be synthesized by a diversity of cells, encompassing both immune and nonimmune populations, its key functions typically involve enhancing cell survival, proliferation, and motility 32 , 50 . Recent advances have delineated the central role of AREG in both bolstering host defense and facilitating tissue restoration during infection 50 . For instance, during influenza infection in the lungs, regulatory T cell-derived AREG mitigates tissue damage and promotes pulmonary repair 51 , 52 , while epithelial cell-produced AREG in response to  Pseudomonas aeruginosa  augments mucin production, facilitating bacterial colonization 53 . Similarly, type-2 helper T cell-derived AREG orchestrates tissue remodeling, improving immune response against intestinal helminthic pathogens 54 , 55 . These studies therefore highlight that AREG production during infection is pathogen-dependent, with distinct cellular sources yielding specific functional outcomes. Our study establishes that within the bone marrow, macrophages surrounding  S. aureus  abscess serve as the primary source of AREG, which promotes the myofibroblast transition of  Adipoq +  cells that are spatially and temporally associated. Notably, emerging evidence suggests a coordination between AREG/EGFR signaling and TGF-β in activating pericyte-myofibroblast transition, stimulating fibroblast proliferation, and inciting the fibroblast-myofibroblast transition in lung and kidney injuries, as well as in pancreatic cancer 48 , 56 – 58 . In our  S. aureus  osteomyelitis model, both transcriptomic and in vitro data demonstrate that AREG is a potent inducer of myofibroblast conversion in preadipocytes. Most importantly, by specific deletion of  Egfr  in  Adipoq +  cells, we have provided definitive in vivo evidence that AREG is essential for triggering myofibroblast transition around  S. aureus  abscess.
L70:Our study uncovers the activation of the AREG/EGFR/mTOR/YAP signaling cascade as a critical mechanism facilitating myofibroblast transition of  Adipoq +  cells within the bone marrow. It is well established that the low-affinity binding of AREG with the EGFR fails to induce receptor internalization, enabling sustained downstream signaling that results in cellular growth arrest and differentiation 50 , 59 . Although extensive research has focused on the specific roles of AREG/EGFR signaling in triggering tissue fibrosis beyond the skeletal system 56 – 58 , 60 – 63 , only two downstream pathways have been identified as regulators of myofibroblast activity, including the MAPK/ERK pathway triggering myofibroblast transition post-microbial intrusion in the mesothelium 60 , and the mTOR pathway influencing myofibroblast proliferation and motility during colitis 61 . In this study, by employing specific inhibitors to screen EGFR downstream pathways, we establish the mTOR pathway as a critical mediator of the myofibroblast transition of  Adipoq +  cells in bone marrow. This finding not only aligns with recent literature of mTOR mediates myofibroblast transition in radiation-exposed MALPs 26 , but also suggests that mTOR may represent a universal regulator of MALPs transdifferentiation across diverse stimuli. Importantly, we extended this understanding by identifying YAP as a principal downstream effector of the AREG/EGFR/mTOR pathway that initiates the myofibroblast transition of  Adipoq +  cells. While accumulating evidence has underlined YAP/TAZ activation in myofibroblasts as a key step in fibrogenesis in kidney, lung, liver, adipose, and skin following injury 64 – 66 , our findings now establish the AREG/EGFR/mTOR/YAP axis as a central regulator of bone marrow fibrosis during  S. aureus  osteomyelitis.
L71:In summary, our study elucidates a fundamental mechanism driving the formation of local fibrosis around  S. aureus  abscess in bone marrow. We demonstrate that  S. aureus  infection activates AREG production in bone marrow macrophages, which then activates the EGFR/mTOR/YAP signaling in neighboring  Adipoq +  cells, driving their transition into myofibroblasts surrounding abscess. Our findings delineate a pathogenic mechanism in osteomyelitis while demonstrating the therapeutic potential of targeting AREG/EGFR/mTOR/YAP to improve antibiotic delivery in  S. aureus  bone infections.
L72:While our study provides important insights, several limitations warrant further exploration. First, the specific macrophage subsets responsible for AREG production and the molecular mechanisms underlying AREG expression regulation in the  S. aureus  abscess microenvironment require further investigation. Second, we cannot exclude potential off-target effects of rapamycin on non-target cells and tissues in vivo, which could confound our findings regarding myofibroblast transition. Finally, considering the crucial role of  Adipoq +  marrow adipogenic lineage precursors in the pathogenesis of  S. aureus  abscess within bone marrow, future studies employing single-cell transcriptome sequencing could provide valuable insights into the fate determination and phenotypic traits of this pivotal cell population.
L78:The implant-associated  S. aureus  osteomyelitis mouse model was developed according to our previously described methodology with minor modifications 27 . Briefly, mice aged 10–12 weeks were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol (125 mg/kg). The right hind limb was shaved and aseptically prepared, followed by blunt dissection through fascia and muscle tissue to expose the femoral surface. A unicortical defect was then created in the third trochanter using a 27-gauge needle. Through this defect, we injected 3 µL of either  S. aureus  suspension (2 × 10 5  CFU/mL) or PBS (sham control) into the femoral medullary using a precise microliter syringe. Subsequently, we insert a sterile, self-tapping steel screw (1.75 mm cap, 1 mm thread with 0.15 mm pitch, 2 mm length) through the same entry point, advancing it in a distal orientation along the femoral medullary cavity. The incision site was then closed with 5–0 surgical sutures. All mice received daily intraperitoneal injection of gentamicin (20 mg/kg) from the first postoperative day, as detailed in our recent work 36 , 72 . Right femoral samples were harvested at day 14 post-infection, when infected femurs displayed profound changes in bone remodeling characteristic of the subacute phase of osteomyelitis 27 , 73 . To assess chronic-stage pathological changes, femoral samples from osteomyelitis models using  AdipoqCre-iDTR  mice and  Cx3cr1Cre-Areg f/f  mice were additionally collected at day 28 post-infection, corresponding to the established chronic phase of osteomyelitis 27 , 28 .
L80:To evaluate the bacterial burden in infected femurs, we aseptically removed all soft tissue from the femurs and recorded their weights. The bones were then homogenized in PBS (1 mL per 0.1 g femoral tissue) using a tissue homogenizer. Bone homogenates were serially diluted to 10⁻⁴, with 100 μL aliquots plated onto tryptic soy agar (TSA) for bacterial culture. For biofilm analysis on implants, we carefully removed screws, rinsed them twice with PBS to eliminate planktonic bacteria and tissue debris, and subsequently subjected them to ultrasonic treatment (40 kHz, 300 W) in 1 mL PBS for 10 minutes to detach the  S. aureus  in biofilm. The solicited suspension was diluted 100-fold, and 100 μL aliquots were plated onto TSA. Following 16 hours of incubation at 37 °C, we enumerated bacterial colonies to determine bacterial CFU.
L81:To assess antibiotic distribution, we intraperitoneally administered FITC-conjugated gentamicin (20 mg/kg, Ruixibio, #R-694). Infected femurs were harvested 1 hour after injection and immediately embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Biosharp Life Sciences, #BL557A) for cryosection. Sections of 15 μm thickness were cut and subsequently imaged with a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM980, Germany) with SoftMax Pro 6 software (version 6.5.1). L94:Paraffin-embedded femurs were sectioned coronally at 4 µm thickness for histochemical analysis. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining was conducted using a commercial kit (Beyotime, #C0105, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. To evaluate the histopathological alterations in infected femurs, we tailored Smeltzer’s scoring system 77 . The adjusted criteria incorporated key features of both clinical osteomyelitis 78  and a mouse model of implant-associated  S. aureus  osteomyelitis 27 . The assessment included a summation of scores across four parameters: intraosseous acute inflammation, characterized by small clusters of neutrophils with possible intramedullary abscess formation; intraosseous chronic inflammation, featuring extensive neutrophilic infiltrates with potential intramedullary fibrosis; periosteal reaction, encompassing periosteal inflammation that may include subperiosteal abscess formation; and bone necrosis, incorporating areas of bone death with or without sequestra present. Each parameter was scored on a scale from 0 (normal) to 4 (most severe manifestations).
