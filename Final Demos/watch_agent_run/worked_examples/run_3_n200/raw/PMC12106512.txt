L1:Diabetic wound healing via a co-delivery system for bone marrow derived stem cells and euphorbia hirta extract: an in vitro and in vivo study
L7:Diabetes mellitus, a persistent metabolic disorder characterized by elevated blood glucose levels, presents a substantial global health challenge due to its escalating prevalence and associated complications. In the current study, a nanofibrous scaffold was developed for the co-delivery of bone marrow-derived stem cells and Euphorbia hirta extract into the diabetic wound beds. Scaffolds were produced using the electrospinning method, and their physicochemical and biological properties were studied in vitro. The wound healing function of the developed delivery system was investigated in a rat model of diabetic wound healing. The study showed that Euphorbia hirta extract-loaded scaffolds imparted no significant toxicity toward L929 cells and promoted their migration activity. A wound healing study showed that scaffolds loaded with both bone marrow-derived stem cells and euphorbia hirta extract had the highest wound healing activity, as shown by significantly higher wound closure percentage, collagen deposition, and epithelial thickness. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent Assay studies showed that these dressings reduced tissue oxidative stress and alleviated inflammatory responses.
L10:Diabetes mellitus, a persistent metabolic disorder characterized by elevated blood glucose levels, presents a substantial global health challenge due to its escalating prevalence and associated complications [ 1 ,  2 ]. A particularly concerning consequence is the impediment of wound healing, notably in individuals with diabetic wounds. These wounds, particularly diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), are a significant complication of diabetes mellitus, resulting from a complex interplay of neuropathy, vascular insufficiency, immune dysfunction, and impaired wound healing mechanisms. These wounds are difficult to treat and often lead to severe outcomes, such as lower-limb amputations [ 3 ,  4 ]. Peripheral neuropathy is a critical factor in the development of diabetic wounds. Chronic hyperglycemia induces oxidative stress and glycosylation of nerve proteins, leading to nerve damage. This damage manifests in sensory, motor, and autonomic dysfunctions, reducing pain perception, causing muscle imbalances, and impairing sweat gland function [ 5 ,  6 ]. Diabetes is associated with peripheral arterial disease (PAD), which exacerbates ischemia in the lower extremities. Hyperglycemia promotes endothelial dysfunction, vasoconstriction, and arterial wall thickening due to atherosclerosis. These changes impair blood flow, reducing oxygen and nutrient delivery to wounds [ 4 ,  7 ]. Additionally, hypercoagulability and reduced vasodilatory responses further hinder circulation. Hyperglycemia disrupts the function of immune cells, such as neutrophils and macrophages. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETosis) become dysregulated in the diabetic environment, leading to chronic inflammation. This proinflammatory state delays wound closure and increases susceptibility to infections [ 8 ,  9 ]. Wound healing involves four overlapping stages: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. In diabetic wounds, this process is disrupted at multiple levels. Chronic hyperglycemia leads to sustained inflammation, excessive extracellular matrix degradation, impaired angiogenesis, and delayed remodeling [ 10 ,  11 ]. Diabetic wounds are prone to infection due to reduced immune defenses and poor circulation. Once infected, these wounds often progress rapidly due to bacterial biofilm formation and tissue necrosis. This can lead to severe complications such as cellulitis or osteomyelitis [ 12 ]. The current landscape of diabetic wound treatment reveals significant limitations in conventional approaches that necessitate the development of innovative therapeutic strategies [ 13 ]. While commercial dressings have made strides in wound management, they often fall short in addressing the multifaceted nature of diabetic wounds [ 14 ]. Existing commercial dressings, such as foam and collagen-based products, offer benefits like moisture control and extracellular matrix (ECM) support. However, they frequently lack the ability to actively modulate the wound microenvironment or promote tissue regeneration [ 15 ]. This shortcoming has led researchers to explore more advanced solutions. Experimental dressings, not yet approved for human use, show promise in targeting specific aspects of diabetic wound healing. These include hydrogels with intrinsic antibiofilm and antioxidant properties and dressings incorporating designer cytokines for precision therapy. Such innovations aim to address the chronic inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, and elevated oxidative stress characteristic of diabetic wounds [ 16 ,  17 ]. Recent advancements in tissue engineering have opened avenues for novel approaches to tackle diabetic wound healing. Among these, electrospun scaffolds have emerged as effective platforms for delivering therapeutic agents and supporting cell-based therapies [ 18 ]. Polymeric materials such as polycaprolactone (PCL) and collagen offer favorable biocompatibility and mechanical properties, making them ideal for scaffold fabrication [ 19 ]. This study concentrates on devising a sophisticated co-delivery system that employs electrospun PCL/collagen scaffolds to concurrently incorporate bone marrow-derived stem cells (BMSCs) and Euphorbia hirta extract, with the aim of enhancing diabetic wound healing.
L18:The ExtractPCLCOL and PCLCOL scaffolds underwent sterilization using 70% ethanol for 60 minutes, followed by several washes with DMEM-F12 media (Invitrogen, USA). Subsequently, L929 cells (Pasteur Institute, Tehran, Iran) were seeded onto the scaffolds at a density of 10,000 cells/cm 2  and cultured for a duration of 8 days. Alamar blue assays were performed on days 2, 4, and 8 to compare cell viability between those cultured on tissue culture plates and those on the scaffolds. In brief, a mixture of 1 ml Alamar blue solution (Invitrogen, USA) with 9 ml DMEM-F12 media containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics was prepared. This solution (350 µl) was applied to the scaffolds and incubated for 4 hours. The absorbance of various samples was subsequently measured at 570 nm using a plate reader (Perkins Elmer, Finland). Each experiment was repeated three times.
L20:L929 cells were seeded on the ExtractPCLCOL and PCLCOL scaffolds at 40,000 cells/cm 2  density in 96-well plates and cultured for 48 hours. Subsequently, a 1% v/v H 2 O 2  solution was introduced into each well, and the cells were then incubated for 60 minutes. Then, the media in each well was discarded, and 350 µL of the Alamar blue solution (Invitrogen, USA) prepared in DMEM-F12 media containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics was added onto the scaffolds and incubated for 4 hours. Finally, the absorbance of the samples was read at 570 nm. Each experiment was repeated three times. Control is the cells cultured on tissue culture plate with DMEM-F12 media containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics.
L32:The in vitro migration of L929 cells in response to ExtractPCLCOL and PCLCOL scaffold extracts was evaluated through a migration assay. In summary, 200 mg of each scaffold was immersed in 10 ml of DMEM-F12 culture media and incubated at 37 °C on a rotary shaker for 5 days. Following incubation, the scaffolds were removed, and the media were filtered. Subsequently, L929 cells were cultivated in 24-well plates until reaching confluence, and a linear scratch was created at the midline of each well using a sterile pipette. After washing away cellular debris with PBS, the cells were exposed to the scaffold extracts for 48 hours. The wound area was photographed on days 0 and 2, and Image J software was employed for the calculation of in vitro wound closure.

L84:Electrospun scaffolds offer a promising approach for delivering cells and bioactive agents to enhance wound healing. These nanofibrous structures provide a conducive environment, promoting cell attachment and controlled release of therapeutic agents, ultimately facilitating tissue regeneration in the wound bed for more effective and accelerated healing processes [ 18 ,  28 ]. In the current research, we developed a delivery system for Euphorbia hirta extract and BMSCs in order to improve diabetic wound healing in a rat model. Our microstructural images revealed a notable similarity between this system and the ECM found in native tissues. This similarity implies that the system has the capacity to replicate the natural environment, potentially influencing cellular behavior to mimic physiological conditions [ 29 ]. Biocompatibility study revealed that none of the extract-loaded or extract-free scaffolds were toxic against fibroblast cells. Besides, the ExtractPCLCOL constructs protected these cells against oxidative stress. This result could be attributed to the presence of euphorbia hirta extract in the matrix of PCLCOL scaffolds. In this context, research conducted on Wistar rats indicated that the euphorbia hirta extract effectively reinstated antioxidant activities and hepatic redox capacity. This suggests a protective impact against oxidative stress induced by carbon tetrachloride [ 30 ]. This result was further validated by the results of DPPH assay that showed that euphorbia hirta extract significantly augmented the radical scavenging capability of PCLCOL scaffolds. The chemical composition of the extract, comprising elements like 2,3-dihydro-3,5-dihydroxy-6-methyl-4H-pyran-4-one, 5-hydroxymethyl-2-furancarboxaldehyde, and 1,2,3-trihydroxybenzene, could play a role in its antioxidant characteristics [ 31 ]. The mechanical behavior of wound dressings affects their handleability during application [ 32 ]. The high ultimate tensile strength of our scaffolds could be due to the presence of PCL in the scaffolds structure [ 33 ]. Bacterial penetration assay showed that ExtractPCLCOL scaffolds significantly reduced bacterial infiltration compared with their extract-free counter parts. It could be that the antibacterial properties of the extract have reduced bacterial growth. The antimicrobial efficacy of the Euphorbia hirta extract is attributed to its chemical composition, comprising elements like flavonoids, tannins, polyphenols, and other phytochemicals known for their antibacterial properties [ 34 ,  35 ]. The water vapor permeability of wound dressings plays a crucial role in minimizing the risk of wound maceration [ 26 ]. The enhanced gas permeability in our developed system may be attributed to the porous structure of electrospun scaffolds. The role of gas permeability in wound healing is crucial, as maintaining an optimal balance of moisture and oxygen exchange is essential for tissue regeneration. In this study, the ExtractPCLCOL and PCLCOL scaffolds demonstrated comparable water vapor permeability, suggesting that the incorporation of Euphorbia hirta extract did not significantly alter this property. The electrospun structure of these scaffolds allows for a high surface area-to-volume ratio and interconnected porosity, which are known to facilitate gas exchange and moisture balance at the wound site. Unlike conventional wound dressings, such as hydrogels and occlusive films, which may restrict gas exchange, electrospun scaffolds provide an environment that supports oxygen diffusion while preventing excessive dehydration. While our study did not directly compare the gas permeability of these scaffolds with that of commercially available wound dressings, existing literature suggests that electrospun materials generally exhibit superior permeability compared to synthetic polymer films or foam-based dressings [ 36 – 38 ]. The adhesion of BMSCs onto the surface of ExtractPCLCOL and PCLCOL scaffolds could be due to the presence of RGD moieties in the structure of collagen [ 39 ]. Our scratch assay implied that Euphorbia hirta extract improved the migration activity of L929 cells. It has been shown that this extract accelerates fibroblast migration activity and Smad-mediated collagen production [ 40 ]. Our wound healing assay showed that the ExtractPCLCOL-BMSCs wound dressings had significantly higher wound healing potential than PCLCOL-BMSCS and negative control groups. We assume that the healing function of euphorbia hirta extract and BMSCs has resulted in the observed healing effects. As shown in our in vitro studies, Euphorbia hirta extract possesses antioxidative capabilities and can protect tissues against oxidative stress. Our ELISA assay results showed that the dressings containing this extract expressed lower amounts of SDs and GPx, indicating that the wound tissue has experienced lower levels of oxidative stress. Downregulation of inflammation might be another possible mechanism by which PCLCOL-BMSCS dressings exert their wound healing effects. Both BMSCs and euphorbia hirta extract are known for their immunomodulatory properties [ 41 ,  42 ]. This theory was further validated by our ELISA assay results that showed the downregulation of IL-6 and TNF-α. TGF-β plays a pivotal role in wound healing by stimulating the production of collagen, promoting cell proliferation, and regulating inflammation [ 43 ]. The upregulation of this cytokine (as shown in ELISA assay) may also contribute to the observed healing effects. We expect that there are potentially various mechanisms behind the observed healing effects that were not elucidated in this research and require further investigations.
