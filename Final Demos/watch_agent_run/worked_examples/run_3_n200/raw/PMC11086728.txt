L1:Cord Blood–Derived Endothelial Progenitor Cells Promote In Vivo Regeneration of Human Hematopoietic Bone Marrow
L9:Victims of acute radiation exposure are susceptible to hematopoietic toxicity due to bone marrow damage and loss of mature blood elements. Here, we evaluated cord blood–derived endothelial progenitor cells (CB-EPCs) as a potential cellular therapy for mitigation of hematologic acute radiation syndrome. CB-EPCs express endothelial cell markers and maintain their growth characteristics beyond 10+ passages without diminishing their doubling capacity. Further, CB-EPCs can be cryopreserved in vapor-phase liquid nitrogen and easily recovered for propagation, making them an attractive nonimmunogenic cellular therapy for off-the-shelf use. Importantly, we show CB-EPCs have the capacity to potently expand adult human bone marrow hematopoietic progenitor cells both in vitro and in vivo.
L11:To demonstrate the role of CB-EPCs in promoting in vivo human immune reconstitution after irradiation, we employed a novel humanized mouse model established by transplant of CD34 +  bone marrow cells from 9 unique adult organ donors into immunocompromised NSG-SGM3 mice. The response of the humanized immune system to ionizing irradiation was then tested by exposure to 1 Gy followed by subcutaneous treatment of CB-EPCs, Food and Drug Administration–approved growth factor pegfilgrastim (0.3 mg/kg), or saline.
L13:At day 7, total human bone marrow was decreased by 80% in irradiated controls. However, treatment with either growth factor pegfilgrastim or CB-EPCs increased recovery of total human bone marrow by 2.5-fold compared with saline. Notably, CB-EPCs also increased recovery of both human CD34 +  progenitors by 5-fold and colony-forming capacity by 3-fold versus saline. Additionally, CB-EPCs promoted recovery of endogenous bone marrow endothelial cells as observed by both increased vessel area and length compared with saline.
L15:These findings indicate the feasibility of using humanized mice engrafted with adult bone marrow for radiation research and the development of CB-EPCs as an off-the-shelf cellular therapy for mitigation of hematologic acute radiation syndrome.
L19:Within the vascular niche, bone marrow endothelial cells critically regulate hematopoietic regeneration after chemotherapy and radiation injury through the release of both hematopoietic growth factors 12 – 14  and extracellular vesicles, 15  which accelerate hematopoietic reconstitution. Bone marrow endothelial cells play a key role in restoring hematopoiesis after myeloablative injury by secretion of hematopoietic growth factors such as granulocyte-colony stimulating factor, epidermal growth factor, pleiotrophin, chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 5, jagged-1, and jagged-2. 6 , 8 , 16 – 19  In an in vitro comparison, endothelial cell co-culture promoted better in vitro regeneration of hematopoietic stem cells than treatment with the established medical countermeasure (MCM) granulocyte-colony stimulating factor alone and was efficacious even when treatment was delayed for 48 hours. 20  However, the bone marrow microenvironment is damaged by radiation injury, and this compromises the ability of the endothelium to support hematopoiesis. 3 , 21  Prior studies have shown infusion of endothelial cells (ECs) alone can accelerate hematopoietic recovery and rescue lethally irradiated mice from death due to H-ARS 9 , 22 – 24  even without engraftment of the transplanted ECs in the marrow.
L20:Endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) represent an attractive potential source of ECs for use as an off-the-shelf cellular therapy, as prior murine allogeneic EPC models have shown infusion of EPCs derived from fetal C57BL/6 mice promotes hematopoietic reconstitution and rescues lethal hematopoietic toxicity in Balb/c recipients. 10 , 25  Here, we establish the feasibility of human cord blood–derived endothelial progenitor cells (CB-EPCs) as an emergency therapy for the mitigation of H-ARS. We demonstrate CB-EPCs retain their phenotypic and functional characteristics after multiple passages and cryopreservation. In vitro, coculture of human hematopoietic stem cells with CB-EPCs protects against radiation-induced apoptotic cell death and promotes expansion of functional HSCs capable of in vivo repopulation. Importantly, we demonstrate CB-EPCs promote in vivo regeneration of human bone marrow CD34 +  hematopoietic cells after radiation injury in a novel humanized mouse model at comparable levels as the FDA-approved mitigator peg-GCSF.
L23:All experimental procedures were carried out according to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of the respective institutions (Medical College of Wisconsin and Duke University). Equal numbers of male and female NSG-SGM3 mice (strain 013062; Jackson Laboratory) were bred for the studies described. The NSG-SGM3 mouse strain was developed from the immunodeficient NOD/SCID-IL2RG strain by co-expressing the human transgenes interleukin - (IL-3), granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, and stem cell factor (SCF). 26  These cytokines support the stable engraftment of myeloid lineages and regulatory T-cell populations. 26  Irradiations were performed between 8 and 10 AM on unanesthetized mice using a SmART Precision x-ray instrument (225 kVP; 10 mA) with a dose rate of 107.7 cGy/min.
L25:Use of deidentified human bone marrow and cord blood units was approved by the institutional review boards of the respective institutions (Medical College of Wisconsin and Duke University). Human bone marrow CD34 +  cells from 9 unique organ donors were purchased from Ossium Health. Ossium Health aseptically recovers human CD34 +  cells from the vertebral bones of organ donors (Ossium Inventa). Research-use-only cells were purchased from 9 donors (5 female and 4 male) representing a range of ages ( Table 1 ). Human cord blood units were obtained from the Carolinas Cord Blood Bank at Duke University or from commercial sources (HumanCells Bioscience, Milpitas, CA). Cord blood units ranged in volume from 40 to 100 mL (median, 85.3 mL).
L27:Male and female 5- to 6-week-old NSG-SGM3 mice were exposed to 2 Gy total body irradiation 24 hours before transplant of human bone marrow cells. Human bone marrow CD34 +  cells (5 × 10 4 ) were then transplanted intravenously into multiple recipient mice, which were sex-matched to the human donor to create a study cohort. Human CD45 +  peripheral blood chimerism was assessed at 6 weeks after transplant by FACS analysis as described in the following sections. For a given study cohort, mice were excluded if their percent human CD45 +  engraftment was greater than 2 standard deviations from the mean. Typically, 3 to 4 mice per cohort were excluded based on this restriction.
L34:Bone marrow vascular imaging
L35:Mice were intravenously labeled with vascular endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin) antibody (25 ug/mouse; BV13-AF647; Biolegend) via intravenous injection as previously described. 31  Fifteen minutes after injection, mice were sacrificed, long bones were harvested, and whole femurs from VE-cadherin–labeled mice were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified for 72 hours in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid/phosphate buffered saline (PBS), incubated for 2 hours in 20% sucrose, and then embedded in optimal cutting temperature medium. Femurs were sectioned at 8  μ m. Sections were rehydrated in PBS, permeabilized in 0.1% Triton, blocked for 1 hour in a solution of 1% bovine serum albumin, and stained for 1 hour at room temperature with antihuman CD34 conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (FAB7277G; R&D Systems). Slides were then rinsed and mounted in ProLong Gold Antifade with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Slides were imaged on an Evos M5000 fluorescent imaging system. Single channel fields (20X) were used for automated vessel analysis using Angio-Tool software. 32  For analysis, pixels with an intensity of 40 (0–255 scale) or less were eliminated as background, and small particles (<64 pixels in diameter) were excluded.
L39:Cryopreserved human bone marrow CD34 +  cells were seeded overnight at a density of 10,000 cells in 0.4 um trans-well inserts (Corning 3413) in Stem span media (Stemcells Technologies) with 1X CC100 supplement (Stemcells Technologies). The following day, CD34 +  cells were exposed to 3 Gy x-ray irradiation using a CellRad instrument. Transwell inserts were then placed in 12-well dishes with CB-EPCs grown to 90% confluence. Media in CB-EPC wells was switched from endothelial growth media to Stemspan with CC100 supplement immediately before transwell placement. At day 7, the progeny of the human bone marrow CD34 +  cells were enumerated on a Cell Countess cell counter with trypan blue exclusion.
L44:Bone marrow analysis
L45:Bone marrow from individual femurs was flushed using a 28-gauge syringe into 5 mL of sterile PBS + 10% fetal bovine serum. Cell pellets were lysed with Ammonium-Chloride-Potassium (ACK) buffer and filtered. Viable cells were enumerated by trypan blue exclusion on a Countess cell counter.
L47:A Miltenyi MacsQuant 10 instrument was used for flow cytometric analysis of bone marrow hematopoietic cell populations. To assess for human hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell populations the following antibodies purchased from Biolegend were used: FITC antimouse CD45 (103108), APC antihuman CD45 (368512), Pacific Blue antihuman Lineage Cocktail (348805; CD3, CD14, CD16, CD19, CD20, CD56), PE antihuman CD34 (343506), Brilliant Violet 510 antihuman CD38 (356612), and 7-AAD Viability Staining Solution (420404). Human lineage analysis was performed using the 7-color immunophenotyping kit (130–098-456; Miltenyi Biotec).
L56:We first assessed whether CB-EPCs were capable of promoting the in vitro expansion of cultured human hematopoietic bone marrow CD34 +  progenitor cells from adult donors either without irradiation (S1) or after exposure to 3-Gy irradiation ( Fig. 2 ). Bone marrow CD34 +  cells were cultured in noncontact conditions with 3 unique CB-EPC lines for 7 days ( Figs. 2A  and  E1A ). At day 7, total viable cells were enumerated ( Figs. 2B  and  E1B ). Without irradiation, coculture with any CB-EPC line dramatically expanded the total number of cells (65- to 68-fold relative to input) and significantly increased the total number of cells relative to treatment with media alone ( Fig. E1B ). Bone marrow CD34 +  cells exposed to 3 Gy and cultured in media alone showed a slight decline in total cell recovery compared with input cell numbers ( Fig. 2B ). However, coculture with CB-EPCs increased the total cell expansion of irradiated CD34 +  cells 8- to 17-fold relative to input ( Fig. 2B ). Compared with the progeny of media control cultures, the progeny of CD34 +  cells cocultured with CB-EPCs had significantly expanded CFC capacity in all nonirradiated coculture assays ( Fig. E1C ) and in 2 of the irradiated CB-EPC coculture assays ( Fig. 2C ). The retention of CD34 expression after in vitro expansion was assessed by FACS analysis ( Figs. 2D  and  E1D ). In media control cultures, cell surface expression of CD34 was largely lost, with, on average, only 10% of nonirradiated cells retaining the CD34 phenotype ( Fig. E1D ). However, coculture of either nonirradiated ( Fig. E1D ) or irradiated ( Fig. 2D ) bone marrow CD34 +  cells significantly increased retention of the CD34 expression. Because coculture with CB-EPCs increased both total expansion and retention of CD34 expression, CB-EPCs significantly increased the fold-expansion of CD34 cells from input in both nonirradiated and irradiated cultures ( Figs. 2E  and  E1E ).
L58:To examine the cellular mechanisms that promote regeneration in HSCs treated with CB-EPCs, we next assessed cell cycle and cell death in bone marrow HSCs in noncontact coculture with CB-EPCs. Here, apoptotic and necrotic cell populations were assessed at days 1 and 7 after 3 Gy in vitro irradiation ( Fig. 2F ) or nonirradiated control culture ( Fig. E1F ). At day 7 in both irradiated and nonirradiated HSC cultures, CB-EPCs reduced the fraction of Annexin V +  7-AAD −  apoptotic cells ( Figs. 2F  and  E1F ). This benefit was pronounced in the irradiated cultures as CB-EPC coculture reduced the apoptotic fraction from 40% in media-treated controls to 10% ( Fig. 2F ). Consistent with their known quiescent phenotype, the majority of bone marrow CD34 +  cells were in the G 0  phase after 24 hours in culture ( Figs. 2G  and  E1G ). By day 7, in nonirradiated cultures ( Fig. E1G ), cells shifted phase from G 0  to G 1  but no treatment-related differences were observed. In irradiated cultures, CB-EPC treatment significantly increased the fraction of cells in G 1  ( Fig. 2G ). Together, these data suggest CB-EPCs promote expansion of human CD34 +  cells primarily by protecting against the induction of apoptotic cell death.
L60:Next, to assess the ability of CB-EPCs to promote in vivo human hematopoietic reconstitution, we developed a mouse xenograft model transplanted with human vertebral bone marrow CD34 +  cells from adult organ donors (N = 9 unique donors). Nine cohorts of humanized NSG-SGM3 (hu-NSG-SGM3) were established by transplant of 50,000 human bone marrow CD34 +  cells into NSG-SGM3 mice conditioned with 2 Gy total body irradiation ( Fig. 3A ). Peripheral blood (PB) chimerism at 6 weeks posttransplant varied by donor, as depicted in  Fig. 3B . For each donor cohort, only mice with human PB chimerism within 2 standard deviations of the mean were selected for inclusion in the study. Characteristics of each donor cohort including donor age, sex, and PB chimerism at 6 weeks are listed in  Table 1 . At 8 weeks after humanization, hu-NSG-SGM3 mice were divided into the following treatment groups: 0 Gy (nonirradiated) control, 1 Gy + PBS injection, 1 Gy + EPC injection, or 1 Gy + peg-GCSF ( Fig. 3C ). The peg-GCSF (Neulasta) treatment group was included to validate the model, as pegGCSF is FDA-approved for the mitigation of H-ARS. At day 7 posttreatment, PB complete blood cell counts (CBCs) were performed on all mice, represented in  Fig. 3B . At day 7 after 1 Gy irradiation, total white blood cell counts (WBCs), the percentage of neutrophils, and platelets were reduced compared with nonirradiated controls ( Fig. 3D ). Consistent with its established function as a hematopoietic mitigator, peg-GCSF treatment significantly increased recovery of WBCs, percentage of neutrophils, and platelets relative to irradiated controls ( Fig. 3D ). CB-EPC treated mice had significantly greater recovery of total WBCs and showed a trend toward increased neutrophil and platelet recovery ( Fig. 3D ).
L61:CB-EPCs promote regeneration of humanized bone marrow in vivo
L62:We next analyzed the bone marrow for recovery of total cellularity and reconstitution of the human bone marrow hematopoietic compartment as assessed by human CD45 FACS expression. Individual data for each donor cohort are included as  supplementary data  ( Fig. E2 ). Total viable bone marrow cellularity including mouse and human cells was decreased after 1 Gy irradiation in all donor cohorts ( Fig. E2A ). However, the fraction of human CD45 +  cells ( Fig. E2B ) and the response of the total human compartment to radiation varied based on human donor ( Fig. E2B ,  E2C ). Therefore, to assess the response of the engrafted human hematopoietic compartment to 1 Gy irradiation and treatment with either PBS, CB-EPCs, or peg-GCSF, the data for each donor cohort were normalized to the respective nonirradiated controls and are represented as fold change relative to nonirradiated ( Fig. 4 ).
L63:The fold change in total bone marrow cellularity normalized to the respective nonirradiated control is shown in  Fig. 4A . On average, total cellularity was decreased 5-fold after 1 Gy radiation exposure at day 7 in PBS-treated controls. Treatment with either CB-EPCs or peg-GCSF consistently increased recovery of total bone marrow cellularity compared with PBS treatment alone ( Fig. 4A ). Within the total viable bone marrow cells, the fraction of human CD45 +  cells was discriminated from murine CD45 +  cells by FACS analysis ( Fig. 4B ). When normalized to the respective nonirradiated control for each sample, the total number of human CD45 +  cells were increased by treatment with either CB-EPCs or peg-GCSF ( Fig. 4B ). Within the human CD45 +  population, treatment with CB-EPCs also increased the number of human CD34 +  progenitor cells identified by FACS analysis ( Fig. 4C ). Consistent with the observed increase in phenotypic progenitor cells by FACS, treatment with either CB-EPCs or peg-GCSF increased the hematopoietic colony-forming potential of human CD45 +  cells isolated from the bone marrow at day 7 ( Fig. 4D ).
L65:To determine whether the reconstitution of PB CBCs is attributable to the murine or human hematopoietic compartments, we next performed a subset analysis of the human and murine stem and progenitor cell compartments of donor cohorts 8 and 9 ( Fig. E3A ). Consistent with the analysis across all the donor cohorts, as shown in  Fig. 4C , 1 Gy irradiation decreased CD34 +  progenitors and completely depleted the HSC-enriched CD34 + CD38 lo  cell population ( Fig. E3A ). Likewise, radiation also decreased murine bone marrow myeloid progenitor cells (lineage negative c-kit +  cell population) and murine hematopoietic stem cells (lineage negative c-kit +  sca-1 +  cell population) ( Fig. E3B ). Interestingly, treatment with peg-GCSF or CB-EPCs promoted reconstitution of both human and mouse progenitor cell populations ( Fig. E3A ,  E3B ). These data suggest that both human and murine cells are likely to contribute to the increase in peripheral blood CBCs observed with peg-GCSF and CB-EPCs. Human lineage analysis was also performed to assess for myeloid versus lymphoid bias after irradiation and treatment ( Fig. E3C ). No differences in the relative fraction of myeloid to lymphoid bone marrow cells were observed ( Fig. E3C ).
L66:CB-EPCs promote regeneration of endogenous bone marrow endothelium
L67:We next assessed structural recovery of the bone marrow vasculature by immunofluorescent imaging. The murine vasculature was labeled before euthanasia by intravenous injection of fluorescently labeled murine VE-cadherin antibody. Bone marrow sections were then counterstained with a human specific antibody for the endothelial marker PECAM (CD31). Consistent with our prior studies, no CD31 +  human endothelial cells were detected in the bone marrow of CB-EPC treated mice, indicating that this allogeneic cell source does not engraft long-term after injection. After 1 Gy, there was visible damage to the integrity of the endogenous bone marrow endothelium compared with 0 Gy control mice ( Fig. 5A ). Vascular area, average vessel length, and the number of vessel junctions were quantified using Angio-Tool software ( Fig. 5A ). 32  Irradiated mice treated with PBS alone observed decreases in all vascular measures relative to nonirradiated controls ( Fig. 5A – D ). However, treatment with CB-EPCs supported the recovery of the endogenous murine endothelium, as evidenced by a significant increase in these measures relative to PBS-treated controls. ( Fig. 5A – D ). Peg-GCSF treated mice had increased recovery of vascular area and a trend toward increased vessel length and junction number ( Fig. 5A – D ).
L69:Umbilical cord blood is a readily available resource, as there are more than 100 public cord blood banks worldwide to cryopreserve donated cord units for potential transplant. 34 , 35  Here, we have established the utility of CB-EPCs as an emergency therapeutic capable of promoting in vivo regeneration of an engrafted human hematopoietic compartment. Consistent with prior studies, 36 – 38  we validated that CB-EPCs are highly proliferative, express endothelial markers, and promote ex vivo hematopoietic expansion. Ingram et al 27  previously demonstrated a single primary CB-EPC could give rise to 10 million progeny, and the expansion potential of this cell source remains remarkably stable over 200 days in culture. These features suggest a possible utility of CB-EPCs for ex vivo HSC expansion. Here, we have shown a single cord blood unit can generate >1 × 10 9  CB-EPCs, which can be cryopreserved for greater than a year without a loss of viability. Thus, this cell source could feasibly be cryopreserved and recovered as needed as an off-the-shelf medical countermeasure for victims of acute radiation syndrome. Additionally, prior murine studies suggest allogeneic EPC treatment is nonimmunogenic and well-tolerated. 10 , 25  Importantly, this study is the first to demonstrate that CB-EPCs promote in vivo regeneration of human hematopoietic progenitors capable of multilineage differentiation. This work was conducted rigorously in a novel humanized mouse model using 9 unique adult bone marrow donors. Although prior humanized models using cord blood CD34 +  progenitor cells have evaluated the in vivo response of a human hematopoietic system to radiation in vivo, 39 – 42  our model is the first to use adult bone marrow CD34 +  cells from multiple donors of varied ages and inclusive of both sexes. Interestingly, donor engraftment and maintenance of the CD34 phenotype were highly variable between donors. This donor-dependent variation in humanization necessitated normalization of the data to the nonirradiated control for each donor cohort. When normalized to the nonirradiated group, we were able to validate the model using the established MCM peg-GCSF (Neulasta). Peg-GCSF treatment reproducibly expanded total bone marrow cell numbers and recovery of human hematopoietic colony-forming potential. Treatment with CB-EPCs had a similar benefit as peg-GCSF treatment. Similar to peg-GCSF, CB-EPCs promoted total cell expansion and CFC numbers, but also significantly increased recovery of the human CD34 population and the endogenous endothelium.
L70:The observation that CB-EPCs promote endothelial recovery is consistent with prior studies in murine H-ARS models. In an allogeneic murine model, CB-EPC infusion after irradiation increased total endothelial cell numbers and reduced bone marrow vascular permeability. 25  Our current data are consistent with this finding, as we saw an increase in murine VE-cadherin +  cells. The enhanced repair of the endothelium in this system may be because of secretion of human angiogenic factors with murine cross reactivity produced by the CB-EPCs. Indeed, endothelial progenitor cells from various sources have been reported to repair multiple types of vascular injuries and have been suggested to have clinical application for treatment of ischemia. 38  For this reason, future studies will address whether CB-EPCs offer mitigation of acute radiation syndrome in other critical organs such as the gastrointestinal tract. Additionally, survivors of ARS are likely to experience progressive inflammatory and fibrotic injury to the vasculature of the lungs and kidneys, which may be mitigated with endothelial cell transplantation. 43
L71:In prior cord blood CD34 +  humanized models of radiation exposure, nonlethal doses of ionizing radiation were observed to induce DNA damage 41  and impair long-term hematopoietic reconstitution. 42  Here, mitigation with either peg-GCSF or CB-EPCs increases acute survival of the human hematopoietic compartment in vivo. Moreover, in vitro assays demonstrate CB-EPCs reduce apoptotic cell death and promote cycling in irradiated hematopoietic progenitor cells. These studies support a role for CB-EPCs in promoting early hematopoietic reconstitution; however, future studies will be necessary to determine the long-term effect of CB-EPC treatment on the HSC pool. Additionally, we observed CB-EPCs have an additive benefit of improved bone marrow vascular recovery relative to treatment with either saline or peg-GCSF.
