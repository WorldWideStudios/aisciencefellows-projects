BugSorter and the BioKEA line

Module-by-module breakdown and the BugSorter → BugDisperser interface spec · Physical AI for Science, Track A · prepared 2026-10-24

Correction first. The BugSorter's output goes to module 2, the BugDisperser, which spreads specimens out on a tray. Module 4 is the enzyme dispenser: a similar name, three steps further down. So the interface to spec is BugSorter output = BugDisperser input.

The dispenser still matters to the sorter, because any liquid and debris the sorter passes on ends up in the dispenser's wells. Section 4 covers that.

What these numbers say.

  • The sorter is not the bottleneck. Sorting a bag takes under an hour, and picking it takes days. Running hands-off, handing over cleanly and leaving nothing behind matter far more than speed.
  • Almost all the work is at the fine end. 83–89% of a Malaise sample is under 3 mm, so the fine end of the sort carries nearly the whole load.

Two gaps nobody owns yet.

  1. Splitting fractions into portions. A fraction holds thousands of specimens and a tray holds about a hundred, so something has to portion fractions and store what's waiting.
  2. The liquid change. Sorting needs specimens submerged: EntoSieve sorts in water, and floaters are its main failure. Flotation dispersal needs high surface tension and specimens at the surface. Those two needs pull in opposite directions.

3. Module 1: BugSorter, piece by piece

What the deck says

  • Job: coarse size sorting using back-and-forth actuation plus water jets. It's the next iteration of the EntoSieve and "module 1 of the line." Deck
  • Current hardware: stacked 3D-printed sieve trays in an acrylic enclosure. Deck
  • Status: it "tentatively works." The challenge is to make it work on real preserved samples, run after run. Deck
  • Kit on the bench:
    • T-slot extrusion in four lengths
    • Mesh sheets in three apertures
    • Stackable round sieve rings that key together with tabs, with three screen types: fine mesh (everything except chia stays on top), perforated plastic (separates rice from couscous) and hex-perforated steel (noodles stay on top)
    • 3D-printed square tray frames in the BugSorter format
    • Perforated steel in two hole sizes, plus a printed honeycomb
    • 12- and 48-well plates for the sorted output
    Deck
  • Open questions on the slide: What motion clears a clogged screen: shaking, tilting, water jets, or all three? How does the stack know it's done? Deck
  • Target for today: three clean size classes from the pantry mix. Deck

The question that decides the weekend. The published EntoSieve sorts at 92–99%, but a person does the loading, the transfers between screens, the unloading and the cleaning. BioKEA's rebuild "never sieved reliably." The cause could be either of two things:

  • Sorting quality (budget parts, different screens, a different stroke). Then copy the paper's settings before inventing anything.
  • The handling around the sieve. Then the motion is already solved, and the real project is unloading, handoff and cleaning.

4. Modules 2–5 and what they demand of the sorter

Module 2: BugDisperser (the sorter's direct customer)

What it does
"Flotation spreads the sorted specimens across a tray so each one sits alone, ready for imaging on the BugPicker." The deck calls it the simplest module and one of the most important. Deck
What it needs from the sorter
Rows 1, 2, 5, 6, 7 and 9 of the interface spec: a carrier, the right liquid, tray-sized portions, loose specimens, little debris, and floaters already dealt with.
Limits
  • Floating objects attract each other. The meniscus pulls them together and toward the walls (the "Cheerios effect"), which works against spreading. Inferred
  • Edges may be out of reach. DiversityScanner can't reach anything within 10 mm of its dish edge. Paper
  • Surface tension is fragile. Ethanol or detergent residue sinks specimens that should float. Inferred
  • Sunk specimens don't spread. Anything that sinks stays where it lands. Inferred
Capacity (worked example)
With a 100 × 100 mm usable area, specimens about 2 mm long and about 5 mm of clear space around each, a tray fits roughly 100–200 specimens at best. Random spreading achieves fewer. At about 25 s per pick, a tray of 100 means a new portion is needed roughly every 40 minutes. Inferred
Ask
Tray size and material; which liquid; what drives the spreading (agitation, flow, air); how many specimens go on a tray; whether the picker takes them off the surface or after they settle. Ask

Module 3: BugPicker

What it does
Vision-guided suction pick-and-place, borrowed from electronics assembly. It puts one specimen in each well of a 96-well plate and links each specimen to its image. Deck
Published analog: DiversityScanner (2022)
  • Handles only specimens under 3 mm, because larger ones don't fit through its pipette tip.
  • Picks with a suction hose and pipette tip driven by a syringe pump.
  • Skips objects that sit too close together until they separate, and re-images the dish after every pick.
  • Classifies specimens into 14 insect groups at about 91% precision.
Paper
What it sets for the sorter
  • The nozzle style decides the classes. If the specimen is sucked up through a tip, the class maximum is the tip's bore. If an electronics-style nozzle seals on the body, it needs a smooth surface, which hairy, winged flies don't have. Inferred
  • One fine tip covers most of a sample. With a cut at 3 mm, a single tip handles 83–89% of a Malaise sample. Paper Inferred
  • Time is tight. The budget is about 25 s per specimen, and every touching pair and every piece of debris uses some of it. Inferred
  • Sorting by type belongs here. Any sorting by appearance happens at this camera, not in the sieve.
Ask
Which nozzle style and bores; the minimum and maximum specimen size; what the 12- and 48-well plates in the kit are for. Ask

Module 4: Enzyme dispenser (not the sorter's input)

What it does
A custom liquid handler on a 3D-printer-style gantry with a syringe. It dispenses lysozyme into every well to start DNA extraction. Its input is filled 96-well plates from the picker. Deck
What it implies upstream
  • Size-matched plates allow per-class volumes. When each plate holds one size class, the protocol can set a volume per class instead of giving a 1 mm and a 5 mm specimen the same amount. Inferred
  • Carried-in liquid can interfere. Liquid arriving with each specimen dilutes the reagent. Ethanol is a known inhibitor of enzymes and PCR, and salt or detergent from flotation may be too. Inferred
  • Check the lysis chemistry. Insect barcoding usually lyses with proteinase K, but the deck says lysozyme. Ask what the chemistry is and whether specimens are meant to survive extraction as vouchers. If they are, sorter damage matters even more. Inferred Ask

Module 5: Turnstile incubator

What it does
Plywood and acrylic built around an off-the-shelf turnstile. Plates rotate through the lysis incubation. Deck
What it implies
Its capacity is set by plate positions and incubation time. With overnight lysis, 12 plates a day needs 12 positions. It makes no direct demand on the sorter. Inferred
Ask
Number of positions, temperature and time. Ask

5. Cross-cutting: rail, arms, software, provenance

  • Rail and ReBot arms. The red arrows on the slide mark the rail between stations. ReBot arms (Chris Anderson) handle plates and trays on that rail. Deck Whatever carries the sorter's output therefore has to be something an arm can grip and set down level without spilling: grip features, a splash wall, a known liquid depth. Inferred
  • Software roadmap. Networked instrument → API/MCP server → agent runs the protocol → provenance written as it runs. Deck For the sorter, that means the API and log in subsystem j.
  • Provenance. The record for each individual specimen begins at the picker's image. Before that, the sorter's batch label is all there is. A specimen carried over between samples is a data error (wrong site), not just contamination. Inferred

7. Direction and limitations

What the BugSorter is for

  • Coarse classes by width, so each tray suits one nozzle, one camera setting and one plate format.
  • Keeping small specimens away from big ones, which crush, hide and shed fragments onto them.
  • Removing human handling, since half the cost per specimen is labor. Deck
  • Starting the batch record that links specimens to their sample.

What it cannot do

  • Separate species. That happens at the picker's camera and the barcode.
  • Fix floaters by running longer. In the paper, doubling the time didn't help. Paper
  • Keep fragments out of the finest class without losing real specimens. Some real specimens are under 1 mm (fairyfly wasps, for example), so a mesh fine enough to catch fragments also catches them. Inferred

Where the real gap is

The published literature has largely solved the sieving motion. What nobody has automated publicly is the handling around it: loading, pulling out giants, moving material between screens, unloading, dealing with floaters, cleaning, and splitting fractions into tray-sized portions. The BugSorter adds value exactly where it removes those human steps.

Recommended order for the weekend

  1. Ask question 1 below (what failed in the rebuild) before building anything.
  2. Build a baseline from the paper's settings on the BugSorter frame: vertical stroke in water, 30–50 mm of water over the screen, perforated metal with 1.5 / 3 / 5 mm holes, maximum speed. Score it with the paper's efficiency formula so your numbers compare directly to its 92–99%:
    • Per class: 100 − (intruders × 100 ÷ specimens that belong in that class).
    • Overall: the average across classes.
  3. Put the design effort into the handling: unloading into a labeled carrier an arm can move, done detection by counting what passes, a floater skim, and the empty check.
  4. Prove "run after run": the same mix five times back to back, with only the automated cleaning in between. Report purity per class, recovery, broken appendages and items left in the machine.

8. Questions to ask Sean or Austin, in order

  1. What exactly failed in the budget rebuild: sorting quality, clogging, damage, or the manual steps around it? (The published version reports 92–99%.)
  2. What does the BugDisperser take in: carrier, liquid, volume, tray size, specimens per tray?
  3. How many specimens are in a typical bag? Does every specimen get barcoded, or only a subsample?
  4. Does the BugPicker suck specimens up through a tip, or seal on them like an electronics nozzle? Which bores, and what size range per nozzle?
  5. What are the 12- and 48-well plates in the kit for: large specimens, or just collecting the demo fractions?
  6. Which cut points do you want? (The paper used 1.5 / 3 / 5 mm.)
  7. Is "back-and-forth" vertical or horizontal, and where do the water jets point?
  8. What is allowed to end up in a well: ethanol, salt, detergent? What is the lysis chemistry, and are specimens kept as vouchers?
  9. Which sample types dominate (Malaise traps? pan traps?), and how long have they sat in 96% ethanol?
